If your machinery is too large for a standard container, should you dismantle it, book special equipment or ship it as breakbulk? The useful answer depends on more than the ocean freight: compare the machinery in its actual shipping condition, the whole transport scope, and what it will take to unload and put it back into service.

This guide draws on an anonymised enquiry about industrial machinery from China to Nigeria. TengYoda discussed a breakbulk option alongside a preliminary container estimate. The enquiry was still being evaluated: the final cargo data, transport method, rate and shipment outcome had not been confirmed. Customer identity, detailed shipment figures and commercial prices have been removed.

## Quick answer: which option should I investigate?

| Option | May be worth checking when | Questions to resolve before choosing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard container | The machine and separately packed parts can enter the door, be loaded and secured, and stay within the equipment and route limits. | What are the final packaged dimensions and weights? Who will dismantle, load, unload and reassemble it? |
| Flat rack or open top | A piece cannot use a standard door or is over height, and the carrier and terminals can accept its size and weight. | Can it be lifted safely? Is any over-gauge space required? What do equipment, securing and handling cost? |
| Breakbulk | The unit is too large for a standard container, or dismantling would add substantial work, and a suitable vessel and terminals are available. | How will it be lifted, stowed and protected? What do the loading terms and destination charges include? |
| RoRo | The equipment can roll on its own wheels or tracks, or an accepted alternative handling arrangement is available. | Will the operator accept its dimensions, condition and proposed method on the required service? |

These are possibilities to request and check, not a promise that every option is available on a particular sailing. Compare quotations for the same cargo condition and handover points before deciding.

## Why total cubic metres alone cannot tell you whether machinery fits

For a standard container, check each complete piece against the door opening, then check how it will be positioned, moved and secured inside. Also check the container payload, concentrated floor load, lifting or forklift access, securing points, and the road limits on the chosen route. A packing plan must allow for skids, protective materials and clearances; adding the machines' volumes is not enough.

For illustration only, [Hapag-Lloyd lists a 40-foot standard high-cube container](https://www.hapag-lloyd.com/en/services-information/cargo-fleet/container/40-standard-high-cube.html) with a door opening of approximately 2.34 m wide and 2.60 m high. A hypothetical machine 2.50 m wide in its intended upright loading position would therefore not pass through that example door unchanged, even if its calculated volume were smaller than the container's capacity. Equipment dimensions vary, so confirm the actual container and any safe alternative loading configuration with the operator.

Ask the manufacturer for the dimensions and gross weight **in the exact condition in which each unit and each loose part will travel**. A statement that a cab has been removed is not enough: identify which machine, where the cab will be packed and what the new complete packing list shows.

## Does dismantling actually save money?

It can, but the decision should include the work at both ends. Obtain the manufacturer's written assessment of what can be removed safely, the resulting dimensions and the required protective packing. Number the pieces and prepare a list, photographs and reassembly instructions.

| Compare before approving dismantling | What to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Dismantling in China | Who performs it, what does it cost, and could it affect warranty or performance? |
| Packing and additional pieces | What are the new dimensions and weights, and how will small parts be identified and protected? |
| Loading and securing | How many containers or special units are required, and who pays for stuffing and lashing? |
| Destination reassembly | Are technicians, lifting equipment, manuals and spare parts available, and who pays for commissioning? |

The lowest ocean rate can be more expensive overall if dismantling, extra handling and reassembly are costly. Conversely, an appropriately dismantled and packed machine may make a container option viable. ## Compare complete costs using the same start and finish points

Request both options against the same cargo list, collection point, destination port or final address, cargo-ready date and agreed responsibilities. A port-to-port rate and a delivered-to-site quotation are not directly comparable.

| Cost stage | Questions for each quotation |
| --- | --- |
| Factory preparation | Are dismantling, waterproofing, rust protection and packing included? |
| China pickup and inland transport | Who arranges loading at the factory, road transport, permits where needed and delivery to the port? |
| Export and origin port | Who is the exporter, who handles declarations, and are port handling, equipment, lashing and loading included? |
| Ocean transport | What vessel or equipment type, rate basis, validity and surcharges apply? |
| Destination port | Is discharge included, and what terminal, storage, transfer or local agent charges remain? |
| Import and delivery | Who appoints the customs broker, pays duties and taxes, transports the cargo and unloads at the receiving site? |
| Reassembly and insurance | Are reassembly, commissioning and insurance quoted separately, and what are the exclusions? |

If a charge is unknown, label it as an allowance or pending quotation rather than treat it as zero. Any actual saving can only be calculated after all relevant costs are confirmed. For a wider costing checklist, read [Breakbulk Project Cargo Costing](/blog/breakbulk-project-cargo-costing-inquiry-quotation/).

## What does RT mean in a breakbulk quotation?

RT means revenue ton, a billing unit defined in the particular quotation. One common weight-or-measure arrangement compares one cubic metre with one metric tonne and charges by the larger number; carriers may instead apply per-piece calculations, minimums, rounding or other terms.

For example only, under that stated rule, one packed piece measuring 8 cubic metres and weighing 5 metric tonnes would be charged as 8 RT before any applicable minimums or surcharges. This is a hypothetical calculation, not the customer's cargo or a rate offer.

Ask whether each piece is assessed separately, whether skids and protrusions count in dimensions, who measures and weighs the cargo, and which final figures and tariff apply. A carrier's actual booking terms govern the charge.

## What does 'including unloading' cover?

In the enquiry, the proposed breakbulk quotation was described as including destination vessel discharge under the quoted terms. This does not establish that every charge after the vessel arrives is included.

Before booking, ask for the handover point in writing: does discharge mean lifting the machine from vessel to quay, and who then handles terminal movement, storage, customs examination, loading onto the buyer's truck and onward delivery? Confirm any port-specific charges with the carrier, terminal and the appointed Nigerian agent. Avoid treating an abbreviation such as FLT as a substitute for a detailed scope.

## How should machinery be protected and insured?

Confirm the proposed stowage with the carrier: on-deck and under-deck exposure may differ, and the final location depends on the accepted booking and vessel plan. The manufacturer and relevant specialists should specify protection for the actual machine and route, including corrosion prevention, openings, vulnerable controls and loose components. Ask who supplies, installs and checks the agreed protection.

Insurance should be quoted for the actual cargo, condition and voyage. Tell the insurer whether each unit is new or used, whether it will be dismantled and whether deck carriage is proposed. Review the policy or certificate, insured value, deductible, exclusions, any deck-cargo conditions, and the claim and survey procedure before dispatch. A document labelled 'all risks' does not by itself confirm cover for every type of loss.
