Yes, you can usually split one purchase order from China into two shipments: send the urgent quantity by air freight and the balance by sea freight. The supplier, forwarder and destination customs broker must agree on the allocation, packing, documents and delivery responsibilities before dispatch. Each shipment needs a traceable record of the goods it actually carries.

This can help an overseas importer bridge a stock shortage without paying air freight on the entire order. It is not automatically the cheapest plan: two shipments may create extra handling, documentation, customs clearance and delivery costs. Compare the complete alternatives before approving the split.

## What does splitting an order actually mean?

One commercial order can have two transport bookings and two arrival dates. For example, an importer purchasing 1,000 identical units might send 200 urgent units by air and 800 by sea. These quantities are illustrative, not a customer case or a recommended fixed ratio.

The split can happen at the factory or at an agreed China warehouse. In either case, the supplier must identify the two batches before they are handed over. The purchase-order number stays useful for reconciliation, while separate shipment references identify the air and sea portions.

This is different from a sea-air service, where the same cargo travels first by sea and then by air on a combined route. Here, different portions of your order move independently by different modes.

## When is an air-and-sea split worth considering?

Consider it when a small urgent batch can solve a specific business problem while the remaining inventory can wait. Common examples include a stockout, replacement parts needed to restore equipment, a launch quantity or an immediate customer order.

It may be less useful if the full order is urgent, the urgent items are bulky or unsuitable for air transport, or the remaining sea batch is so small that minimum charges dominate the cost. Arrival uncertainty also matters: airport-to-airport transit is not the same as the date stock becomes available at your premises.

Ask which quantity you need **usable at the destination**, by which date, and what happens if it arrives later. Avoid choosing the air quantity simply because it is a round percentage of the order.

## How many units should go by air?

Start with demand and inventory rather than the freight rate alone:

**Urgent units = expected demand until the sea batch is usable + agreed safety stock − usable stock already available − other confirmed incoming stock.**

For an illustrative planning exercise, demand of 10 units a day over a 25-day gap requires 250 units. Adding 30 units of safety stock and subtracting 80 usable units already available leaves 200 units to bridge the gap.

The 25 days is an assumption for the example, not a promised shipping time. Use the expected sea delivery date after unloading, clearance and final delivery, then allow for uncertainty. Also check carton quantities: if a carton contains 24 units, opening or repacking may be needed to dispatch exactly 200.

## A practical workflow from supplier to destination

### 1. Confirm the commercial and transport scope

Provide the purchase-order reference, supplier contacts, SKU list, quantities, product values, cargo-ready dates and destination addresses. Confirm that partial deliveries are permitted under the purchase contract. If payment uses a letter of credit or another documentary arrangement, have the relevant parties check its partial-shipment conditions before booking.

Confirm the applicable trade terms and who pays each stage. Splitting a shipment does not automatically change an EXW or FOB sales agreement. Read [EXW vs FOB from China for small importers](/blog/exw-vs-fob-from-china-small-importers/) if responsibilities are unclear.

### 2. Create a shipment allocation sheet

Use one master record, with separate columns for each dispatch. An illustrative allocation might look like this:

| Item | Total order | Air batch | Sea batch | Control check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SKU A | 1,000 units | 200 units | 800 units | Air + sea = total order |
| Shipment reference | PO-1001 | PO-1001-AIR | PO-1001-SEA | Keep references on records and cartons |
| Cartons | Confirm after packing | Record actual count | Record actual count | Match each batch's packing list |
| Packed size and weight | Record both batches | Measure air packages | Measure sea packages | Calculate each mode separately |

Specify which accessories, spare parts and manuals belong with each batch. Do not send the main product by air while leaving essential components in the sea shipment.

### 3. Agree where the goods will be separated

Factory separation can avoid reopening mixed cartons at a warehouse. Warehouse separation can help when goods come from multiple suppliers or when final measurements are still needed. Confirm labour, opening-carton permission, packing materials, storage and reporting charges in advance.

### 4. Pack and identify each batch

Air and sea cargo can need different protective packaging and handling arrangements. Measure the final outside dimensions, including pallets and crates, after repacking. Air freight is generally assessed against gross and volumetric weight under the quoted service's rules; reducing empty space safely may therefore matter.

Use separate physical storage zones and clear batch references. Carton numbering such as AIR 1 OF 10 and SEA 1 OF 40 can help prevent cross-loading when agreed with the receiving warehouse. These internal identifiers do not replace carrier labels, dangerous-goods marks or mandatory product markings.

Keep package-level records and photos where agreed. For more detail, see [shipping marks for China consolidation](/blog/shipping-marks-for-china-consolidation/) and [warehouse receiving checks](/blog/freight-forwarder-check-goods-china/).

### 5. Prepare documents that match each shipment

Each batch needs shipping and customs information reflecting its actual products, quantities, values, packages and weight. Do not give both clearances a document showing the full order value and quantity without explaining the split to the broker.

Separate shipment invoices and packing lists are often the clearest arrangement. If the supplier uses one master commercial invoice, ask the export agent and destination broker what shipment-specific breakdowns and references they require. Local rules and the commercial arrangement determine the acceptable format.

| Record | Air portion | Sea portion |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial information | Actual air quantity and properly allocated value | Actual sea quantity and properly allocated value |
| Packing list | Air cartons, contents, dimensions and weights | Sea packages, contents, dimensions and weights |
| Transport document | Air waybill under the booked service | Bill of lading or sea waybill under the booked service |
| Customs information | Batch-specific declaration data as required | Batch-specific declaration data as required |
| Tracking record | Air dispatch and delivery milestones | Sea dispatch and delivery milestones |

Allocate prices, discounts and any relevant charges consistently, with broker guidance. Keep the combined quantities and values reconcilable to the order. Splitting transport is not a reason to undervalue goods or avoid duties and taxes. See [documents required for shipping from China](/blog/documents-required-shipping-from-china/).

### 6. Check cargo acceptance for both modes

Acceptance by a sea provider does not establish acceptance by an airline. Disclose batteries, liquids, chemicals, powders, magnets and other special contents before requesting the air booking. Provide product specifications and relevant transport documentation for review; a generic description such as “accessories” can conceal important restrictions.

Battery and dangerous-goods requirements depend on classification, configuration, packing and the selected service. Appropriate specialists must confirm current rules and carrier acceptance. Do not assume an SDS alone proves that a shipment can travel by air, or that every battery shipment requires the same declaration.

For product screening, read [can my product be exported from China?](/blog/can-my-product-be-exported-from-china/).

### 7. Approve release and track both deliveries

Before dispatch, reconcile the allocation sheet with actual carton counts and documents. Confirm booking acceptance, payment requirements, handover cutoffs and the person authorised to release each batch.

Track factory readiness, pickup, origin handover, departure, destination clearance and final delivery separately. Once the air batch is received, reconcile delivered quantities and report discrepancies while the records remain easy to trace. Repeat this for the sea batch and close the master order only after both are accounted for.

## How to compare the total cost

Request three options using the same cargo and delivery scope: all sea, all air, and a split. Compare costs to the agreed destination, including conditional expenses and timing assumptions.

**Split transport cost = air-batch cost + sea-batch cost + extra separation, packing, storage and coordination charges.**

Then consider the business value of earlier usable stock separately. Avoid presenting avoided lost sales as a guaranteed freight saving.

| Cost question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Does each batch incur a minimum freight charge? | A smaller remaining sea batch may still hit an LCL minimum |
| Are two collections needed? | Separate pickup dates can duplicate trucking costs |
| Are documentation or clearance fees charged twice? | Two dispatches can create separate shipment-level fees |
| Is repacking required? | Labour and materials can change both cost and chargeable size |
| Does each batch require final delivery? | Address access and unloading requirements apply to each delivery |
| Is one supplier late? | Holding the sea batch can create storage or missed-cutoff costs |

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I split an order after the supplier has packed everything?

Often yes, if cartons can be identified and safely separated before dispatch. Opening mixed cartons, repacking and revising documents may add cost and time. Once cargo is booked or handed over, changes require the relevant provider's approval and may be impractical.

### Can the air and sea batches come from different suppliers?

Yes, subject to an agreed consolidation and documentation plan. Keep each supplier and SKU traceable. Multiple suppliers do not automatically mean one invoice, one declaration or one fixed fee.

### Can both batches use the same purchase-order number?

Yes, as a master commercial reference. Use separate shipment references and accurate batch-specific records so the warehouse, forwarder and broker can distinguish them.

### Will the air batch definitely arrive first?

Air is typically chosen for speed, but readiness, acceptance, flight availability, customs and delivery can affect the result. Confirm realistic door-delivery estimates for both batches and avoid promising a guaranteed date without a service commitment.

### Can I use express for the urgent batch instead?

Yes, if the cargo and destination are accepted. Ask TengYoda to compare [international express](/services/international-express/) with air freight using the same package sizes, weight, delivery address and import responsibilities.
